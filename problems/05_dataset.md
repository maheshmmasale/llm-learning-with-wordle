# Problem 05 — Building a Supervised Dataset from an Entropy Solver

## Objective

Build a reproducible data-generation pipeline that produces **at least 100,000 validated supervised examples** for training a model to choose the next guess in a hidden-target deduction game. Each example must map a legal game state to a strong or optimal next guess generated by an entropy-based reference solver. The final dataset should represent the full progression of play rather than only opening positions, should expose both common and difficult states, and should be suitable for later supervised fine-tuning without train/test contamination.

This is a dataset-engineering assignment, not merely a request to dump solver traces. Your pipeline must define the unit of supervision, justify how states are sampled, distinguish truly optimal labels from approximate strong labels, detect malformed examples, remove duplicates, and produce deterministic splits. A successful submission should make it possible for another researcher to regenerate the corpus, audit any record, and understand exactly what population of states the reported results describe.

## Background

In a hidden-target guessing game, a state is determined by the legal target universe, the sequence of prior guesses and feedback, and the remaining candidates consistent with that history. A reference policy can evaluate each legal next guess by partitioning the remaining candidates according to possible feedback. Common scores include expected posterior entropy, expected remaining candidate count, worst-case bucket size, and probability of solving immediately. An entropy solver is often strong, but its output depends on details such as whether non-candidate probe guesses are allowed, how ties are resolved, and whether targets are weighted uniformly.

Training records created from sequential rollouts are highly correlated. The same early state may appear thousands of times, and the same underlying puzzle or target can leak across splits through nearly identical histories. Random row-level splitting therefore gives misleading evaluation numbers. Your corpus must be organized around independent groups, such as target instances, generated puzzle universes, or canonical state families, so that held-out data measures generalization instead of memorization.

## Exact Requirements

1. Implement a command-line dataset generator with explicit seed, output directory, requested example count, solver configuration, and split configuration. The invocation and all effective parameters must be recorded in a manifest.
2. Generate no fewer than **100,000 unique state-to-next-guess examples after validation and deduplication**. The count before filtering does not satisfy the requirement.
3. Use an entropy-based reference solver to label every retained state. For tractable states, enumerate all legal guesses and compute exact scores. For larger states, approximation is permitted only if the record is marked accordingly and includes the approximation budget. Define “optimal” and “strong” precisely in metadata.
4. Include diverse states across turn number, remaining-candidate count, feedback-history length, solver margin, candidate/non-candidate best guesses, and difficulty. Do not allow opening positions or a narrow candidate-count band to dominate the corpus.
5. Define a versioned, machine-readable record format, preferably JSONL or Parquet. Every row must contain at least: schema version, state identifier, game/rules identifier, legal action space or a stable reference to it, canonical history, remaining candidates or an auditable representation, selected label, label score, tie information, exact-versus-approximate status, generation seed/provenance, and split/group identifier.
6. Canonicalize states before hashing. Two histories that imply the same information state must be treated consistently. Explain whether they are considered duplicates and why.
7. Implement validation for legal guesses, legal feedback, history consistency, nonempty candidate sets, label legality, score recomputation on a sample, stable serialization, and correct solver tie handling. Invalid rows must be rejected and counted by reason.
8. Deduplicate before splitting. Report exact duplicate counts and, if used, near-duplicate or equivalence-class counts.
9. Produce deterministic train/validation/test splits with a documented grouping key. Prevent the same target instance, puzzle universe, or canonical equivalent state family from crossing split boundaries. Add an automated leakage audit.
10. Store dataset-level metadata: row counts, split counts, feature histograms, solver version or source revision, wall-clock time, CPU/GPU usage if any, and checksums for all released files.
11. Provide a lightweight loader and a verification command that checks schema validity, hashes, split exclusivity, minimum count, and a random sample of labels.
12. Do not include any trained model or downstream solution in this assignment. The deliverable is the dataset pipeline, dataset, and evidence of quality.

## Acceptance Criteria

- The validated corpus contains at least 100,000 unique examples and all three splits are nonempty.
- Running the generator twice with the same configuration and seed yields identical manifests, split assignments, and content hashes, apart from explicitly excluded timing fields.
- The reference solver can recompute labels and scores for a sampled audit set within documented numerical tolerance.
- Automated tests intentionally inject malformed histories, impossible feedback, illegal labels, duplicates, and cross-split group collisions, and each failure is detected.
- No grouping unit selected for leakage prevention occurs in more than one split.
- Dataset statistics demonstrate meaningful coverage across early, middle, and late game states and across multiple difficulty bands.
- Documentation makes all assumptions—including target prior, legal guesses, score definition, tie policy, and approximation policy—unambiguous.

## Expected Experiments

Run at least three state-generation strategies, such as complete solver rollouts, randomized-policy rollouts followed by expert relabeling, and targeted sampling by remaining-candidate count. Compare their coverage and redundancy. Evaluate at least two weighting or balancing schemes and show how they alter the state distribution. Measure solver-label stability under alternate deterministic tie breakers and, where approximation is used, compare approximate labels against exact enumeration on a feasible subset. Conduct an ablation in which deduplication or group-based splitting is replaced by naïve row splitting; quantify how much apparent validation performance or state overlap changes, without using the naïve result as the final benchmark.

## What to Measure/Metrics

Report total generated, rejected, deduplicated, and retained rows; unique canonical states; exact and approximate label fractions; split sizes; leakage violations; label-recomputation agreement; and generator throughput. Include histograms or quantiles for turn index, remaining candidate count, legal action count, entropy score, top-two score margin, and number of tied optima. Measure action-label frequency, fraction of labels outside the remaining candidate set, state-family concentration, and effective sample size under duplicate grouping. For approximation, report action agreement, score regret, and rank correlation relative to exact computation. Include dataset file sizes and checksum verification results.

## Questions to Answer

1. What precisely constitutes the state observed by the model, and what information is intentionally excluded?
2. Under what assumptions is the entropy-maximizing guess considered optimal, and when is it only a strong heuristic?
3. How do you prevent repeated trajectories or equivalent information states from inflating dataset size?
4. What grouping key provides the strongest practical defense against leakage, and what generalization claim does the chosen split support?
5. Which regions of the state space remain underrepresented after balancing?
6. How often are there multiple equally scored labels, and how should a learner be evaluated on those states?
7. What evidence shows that approximate labels are reliable enough to retain?
8. If the corpus were expanded by an order of magnitude, which component would become the computational bottleneck and why?
